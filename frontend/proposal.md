
# STRAYSAFE

## Comprehensive System and Business Process Proposal

### Proposed Animal Incident Reporting, Community Coordination, and Case Management System

**Document Type:** System and Business Process Proposal
**Purpose:** Stakeholder Consultation, Requirements Validation, and Proposed Workflow Review
**Status:** Proposed — Subject to Stakeholder Approval
**Target Environment:** Residential Subdivision
**Primary Stakeholders:** Subdivision Management, Residents, and Relevant External Authorities

---

# 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

StraySafe is proposed as a centralized platform for reporting, assessing, coordinating, monitoring, and documenting animal-related concerns within a residential subdivision.

The system aims to address the limitations of informal reporting methods, particularly reports submitted through unofficial social media groups or direct communication with subdivision management.

Under existing informal arrangements, residents may report stray animals, lost pets, animal-related nuisances, or safety concerns. However, these reports may not consistently receive acknowledgment, appropriate coordination, progress updates, or documented outcomes.

StraySafe seeks to improve this process through structured case management and community participation.

The system is not intended to replace the responsibilities of barangay authorities, veterinary personnel, wildlife responders, or other authorized organizations.

Instead, it supports the subdivision management in coordinating appropriate responses while maintaining responsibility for managing animal-related cases within the subdivision.

### Proposed Core Principle

**Every valid animal-related concern should have a documented assessment, an appropriate response or disposition, and traceable follow-through.**

This does not mean that every reported animal must be captured, rescued, or physically removed.

Rather, the system should help ensure that concerns are addressed proportionately according to their nature, urgency, available evidence, applicable policies, and the subdivision's actual operational capabilities.

# 2. BACKGROUND AND PROBLEM IDENTIFICATION

## 2.1 Existing Reporting Environment

Based on preliminary stakeholder information, animal-related concerns are currently communicated through informal channels, including:

- Unofficial subdivision Facebook groups.
- Direct communication with the subdivision leader.
- Personal communication between residents.
- Coordination with the barangay when subdivision-level intervention is insufficient.

Some concerns may be assessed by the subdivision leader or designated personnel.

When an incident requires assistance beyond subdivision capabilities, coordination with the barangay may occur.

However, the exact procedures for handling unidentified or ownerless animals still require confirmation.

## 2.2 Identified Problems

### A. Fragmented Reporting

Animal-related concerns are distributed across different communication channels.

This makes it difficult to maintain organized records and monitor recurring incidents.

### B. Uncertain Report Acknowledgment

Residents may not know whether their concerns have been received or reviewed.

### C. Limited Progress Visibility

After submitting a report, residents may have no reliable way to determine whether subdivision management has taken action.

### D. Inconsistent Case Documentation

Reports, inspections, referrals, warnings, and outcomes may not be consistently recorded in a centralized location.

### E. Difficulty Identifying Animals and Owners

Animals may have similar physical appearances, move between locations, or lack reliable ownership records.

Consequently, identifying a specific animal or its owner may be difficult or impossible using the available information.

### F. Unclear Coordination and Follow-through

Even when an incident is referred to an external authority, the subdivision may still need to monitor whether the original concern has been appropriately addressed.

### G. Limited Community-Assisted Recovery

Lost-pet information and sightings may be shared informally without a structured way to consolidate relevant updates.

## 2.3 Proposed Problem Statement

The subdivision lacks a centralized and consistently documented mechanism for receiving, assessing, coordinating, and monitoring animal-related concerns, resulting in fragmented information, limited case visibility, and difficulties maintaining accountability throughout the response process.

# 3. PROPOSED SYSTEM OBJECTIVES

## 3.1 General Objective

To develop a centralized animal incident reporting and coordination system that supports subdivision management in organizing animal-related concerns, facilitating community participation, coordinating appropriate interventions, and monitoring cases until a documented disposition is reached.

## 3.2 Specific Objectives

The proposed system aims to:

1. Provide residents with an accessible method for reporting animal-related concerns.
2. Enable subdivision management to review, verify, categorize, and prioritize reports.
3. Support community awareness of lost pets and unidentified animals.
4. Allow residents to submit additional sightings and relevant information without unnecessarily creating separate incidents.
5. Maintain historical location observations without implying real-time animal tracking.
6. Support evidence-based animal identification and ownership assessment.
7. Facilitate applicable owner warning and compliance procedures.
8. Document coordination with barangay authorities and other appropriate organizations.
9. Maintain subdivision-level case monitoring even when external assistance is involved.
10. Provide residents with meaningful case progress updates.
11. Preserve incident history, supporting evidence, and significant administrative decisions.
12. Support appropriate case closure based on documented outcomes.

# 4. SYSTEM SCOPE AND BOUNDARIES

## 4.1 Geographic Scope

StraySafe primarily operates within the participating subdivision.

Its operational responsibilities are limited to animal-related concerns affecting that subdivision.

Reports involving animals observed near or beyond subdivision boundaries may be documented when relevant to an existing case.

However, the system must not assume that subdivision personnel are authorized to conduct interventions outside their jurisdiction.

Such situations may require coordination with the appropriate external authority.

## 4.2 Animal Coverage

StraySafe should not be restricted exclusively to dogs and cats.

The core reporting system should accommodate different animal types, including:

- Dogs.
- Cats.
- Birds.
- Reptiles.
- Livestock.
- Other domestic animals.
- Unidentified or other animals.

The animal type provides context for assessment and response.

It should not require a completely separate reporting system for each species.

### Important Limitation

The system should not attempt to independently diagnose animal diseases, determine whether an animal is venomous, or guarantee species identification through artificial intelligence.

Potentially dangerous animals should be handled through appropriate safety assessment and coordination with trained personnel.

## 4.3 Included Functional Areas

The proposed system includes:

1. Animal Incident Reporting.
2. Community Lost-and-Found Notices.
3. Incident Case Management.
4. Sighting and Location Updates.
5. Optional Pet Registry.
6. Animal and Ownership Assessment.
7. Owner Warning and Compliance Monitoring.
8. External Referral and Coordination Tracking.
9. Resident Notifications and Case Updates.
10. Case History and Outcome Documentation.

## 4.4 Excluded Responsibilities

StraySafe is not intended to:

- Physically capture animals.
- Operate an animal shelter.
- Assume animal custody.
- Provide veterinary treatment.
- Diagnose rabies or other diseases.
- Guarantee animal rescue.
- Independently determine legal ownership.
- Impose or collect barangay fines.
- Replace government animal control procedures.
- Manage the internal operations of external authorities.
- Provide real-time animal GPS tracking.

These activities remain outside the proposed subdivision-level software scope.

# 5. PROPOSED USER ROLES

## 5.1 Resident

Residents may:

- Submit animal-related reports.
- Report missing pets.
- Publish found-animal notices.
- View relevant community notices.
- Submit possible animal sightings.
- Track their submitted reports.
- Provide additional evidence or information.
- Register pets voluntarily.
- Submit ownership claims.
- Respond to applicable warnings.
- Confirm recovery of their missing pets.

Resident permissions should be limited to their own records and community information appropriate for public visibility within the subdivision.

## 5.2 Subdivision Administrator

The subdivision head or authorized subdivision personnel may:

- Review incoming reports.
- Assess incident urgency.
- Verify available evidence.
- Request additional information.
- Identify potentially related reports.
- Assess possible animal ownership.
- Coordinate with residents.
- Record applicable warnings.
- Monitor compliance.
- Coordinate with external authorities.
- Document intervention outcomes.
- Maintain case histories.
- Determine appropriate case disposition.
- Close subdivision cases.

For the initial proposal, these responsibilities may be implemented under one administrative role.

Additional internal roles should only be introduced when required by actual subdivision operations.

## 5.3 External Authorities

External authorities may include:

- Barangay personnel.
- Authorized animal control personnel.
- Veterinary offices.
- Appropriate animal welfare organizations.
- Wildlife response authorities.

These organizations are external participants in coordination activities.

**A separate external authority account is not required for the initial system.**

The subdivision administrator may record communications and outcomes received from these organizations.

# 6. FUNDAMENTAL BUSINESS PRINCIPLES

The following principles should guide the entire system.

## Principle 1: Incident-Centric Design

StraySafe should primarily manage animal-related incidents rather than attempt to maintain definitive identities for every animal observed.

An incident can be valid even when the animal or its owner remains unidentified.

## Principle 2: Reporting Does Not Require Perfect Evidence

Residents should not be required to approach potentially dangerous animals to obtain photographs or identifying details.

Incomplete but relevant reports should still be accepted for appropriate assessment.

## Principle 3: Observation Is Not Identification

A photograph, description, or GPS location does not necessarily establish the identity of a specific animal.

## Principle 4: Registration Is Not Proof of Ownership

Pet registration provides supporting information but does not independently establish legal ownership.

## Principle 5: Matching Is Not Confirmation

Physical similarities and AI-generated suggestions must not automatically establish animal identity or ownership.

## Principle 6: Verification Must Be Evidence-Based

Administrative conclusions should reflect the available evidence and the subdivision's applicable procedures.

Uncertainty must remain visible when evidence is insufficient.

## Principle 7: Community Communication Should Remain Natural

Residents should not be forced to submit formal reports for information already communicated directly to an owner.

The system should allow relevant information to be recorded afterward.

## Principle 8: External Referral Does Not Automatically Resolve a Case

A subdivision case should remain subject to appropriate monitoring until a documented disposition is reached.

## Principle 9: Operational Decisions Remain Human Responsibilities

The system should support decision-making rather than independently determine punishment, ownership, animal custody, or intervention outcomes.

## Principle 10: Responses Must Be Proportionate

Not every animal-related concern requires physical removal or external intervention.

The response should depend on the reported concern, urgency, available evidence, applicable policies, and authorized operational procedures.

# 8. ANIMAL INCIDENT REPORTING

## 8.1 Report Categories

The proposed reporting interface should support the following concerns:

| Category                    | Description                                                 |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Lost Pet                    | An owner is searching for a missing animal                  |
| Found / Unidentified Animal | An animal has been observed, but its owner is unknown       |
| Animal Nuisance             | An animal is causing repeated disturbance or inconvenience  |
| Animal Safety Concern       | An animal may pose a threat to people or other animals      |
| Injured / Distressed Animal | An animal may require appropriate welfare assistance        |
| Other Animal Concern        | A concern not adequately represented by existing categories |

Categories may overlap.

The system should allow relevant secondary concerns to be recorded without forcing users to submit duplicate reports.

## 8.2 Suggested Report Information

A standard report may include:

- Report category.
- Animal type.
- Description.
- Photograph or video, when available.
- Observed location.
- Observation date and time.
- Description of the incident.
- Relevant behavior or safety concerns.

The system should distinguish required information from optional supporting evidence.

Photographs must not be mandatory when obtaining them would be unsafe.

## 8.3 AI-Assisted Description

The existing AI-assisted description feature may be retained as an optional convenience.

It may suggest observable animal characteristics based on an uploaded image.

However:

- Suggestions must be reviewable and editable.
- AI output must not automatically establish animal identity.
- AI output must not establish ownership.
- AI output must not diagnose medical conditions.
- Report submission must remain possible when AI is unavailable.

The system should remain functional without AI.

# 9. LOCATION CAPTURE AND ANIMAL MOVEMENT

## 9.1 Proposed Location Method

StraySafe should support:

**GPS-assisted location capture with manual map correction.**

When submitting a report, the system may request permission to obtain the device's current coordinates.

The suggested location should appear on a map.

The resident must be able to adjust the location pin before submission.

If GPS is unavailable or inaccurate, the resident may manually select the observed location.

## 9.2 Photograph Location Metadata

When available, photograph metadata may provide a possible location reference.

However, photograph metadata should not automatically be treated as reliable evidence of the animal's observed location.

The photograph may have been captured earlier or elsewhere.

The preferred approach is to use device location as a suggestion and require the resident to confirm the actual observation location.

## 9.3 Separate Observation and Submission Times

The system should distinguish:

**Observed At:** When the animal was seen.

**Reported At:** When the information was submitted.

**Observed Location:** Where the animal was reportedly seen.

This distinction is important when residents submit delayed reports.

## 9.4 Moving Animals

Animals may move after being reported.

Therefore:

**A location pin represents a historical observation, not the animal's guaranteed current location.**

Example:

| Observation         | Location | Time    |
| ------------------- | -------- | ------- |
| Initial report      | Street A | 9:00 AM |
| Additional sighting | Street B | 9:20 AM |
| Additional sighting | Street C | 9:45 AM |

The system may display the latest reported sighting.

However, the current animal location should remain unknown unless there is a sufficiently recent observation—and even then, the observation is not proof that the animal remains there.

## 9.5 Unsuccessful Site Visits

If authorized personnel visit the reported location and the animal is no longer present, the result should be recorded.

Example:

**Inspection Result:** Animal Not Found

This must not automatically close the case.

Further monitoring, coordination, or closure may be appropriate depending on the circumstances.

# 10. COMMUNITY LOST-AND-FOUND MANAGEMENT

Lost-and-found activities should support community participation without requiring subdivision approval for every routine interaction.

## 10.1 Lost Pet Reporting

A resident who loses a pet may submit:

- Pet name, if applicable.
- Animal type.
- Physical description.
- Recent photograph.
- Last-known location.
- Last-seen date and time.
- Other relevant identifying information.

The resident may select an existing pet profile or enter the details manually.

**Prior pet registration should not be required.**

## 10.2 Automatic Lost-Pet Publication

The proposed default is to publish a lost-pet notice immediately after submission.

The notice should be labeled as owner-reported information rather than an officially verified subdivision finding.

This avoids unnecessary delays while the owner searches for the animal.

Subdivision management may moderate inappropriate or abusive notices.

Automatic publication remains subject to stakeholder approval and applicable subdivision communication policies.

## 10.3 Found / Unidentified Animal Notices

A resident who observes an animal with an unknown owner may submit a found-animal notice.

The notice may become visible to other residents without waiting for an administrator to identify the animal.

This supports possible owner identification and community assistance.

However, the report should also be available to subdivision management for assessment when appropriate.

## 10.4 Publication Versus Official Action

Three operations must remain distinct:

**Report Submission:** Information enters the system.

**Community Publication:** Relevant information becomes visible to residents.

**Official Action:** Subdivision management assesses and responds to a concern.

A published notice does not automatically mean the subdivision has verified the report.

Likewise, an official response should not depend entirely on whether a community notice has received attention.

## 10.5 Community Notification Strategy

Automatic publication does not require sending a push notification to every resident for every report.

A proposed approach is:

| Event                    | Proposed Handling                                                |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Lost-pet report          | Publish notice and send appropriate community alert              |
| Found-animal report      | Publish notice; notification may depend on relevance             |
| Additional sighting      | Notify relevant case participants                                |
| Owner-confirmed recovery | Update notice and notify relevant participants                   |
| Important admin update   | Notify affected or relevant residents                            |
| Urgent safety concern    | Prompt administrative alert and appropriate safety communication |

The system should avoid excessive or repetitive notifications.

# 11. LOST-PET SIGHTINGS AND RECOVERY

## 11.1 Direct Communication Between Residents

Residents may communicate outside StraySafe.

For example:

A pet owner reports a missing dog.

A neighbor sees the dog and immediately informs the owner in person.

The neighbor should not be required to create a separate system report.

Instead, the owner may update the existing lost-pet case.

## 11.2 Owner-Submitted Sighting Updates

An owner may record:

- New possible location.
- Observation time.
- Additional description.
- Information source.

The system should distinguish:

- Personally observed.
- Reported by another person.

This helps preserve the reliability and origin of information.

## 11.3 Bystander-Submitted Sightings

A resident may submit a possible sighting associated with an existing lost-pet case.

The sighting should remain unconfirmed unless sufficient evidence supports the identification.

The resident should not be required to approach or capture an animal.

## 11.4 Possible Matching

The system may suggest potentially matching lost-pet records using available information.

Matching may consider:

- Species.
- Physical characteristics.
- Distinctive markings.
- Photographs.
- Observation location.
- Observation time.

However, these factors provide possible associations, not definitive identification.

## 11.5 Recovery Confirmation

The pet owner should be able to confirm that the missing pet has been recovered.

For ordinary owner-confirmed recovery, subdivision administrator approval should not be mandatory unless an actual operational requirement justifies it.

Once recovery is confirmed, the lost-pet notice may be marked as recovered and closed.

The original report and relevant sighting history should remain preserved.

## 11.6 Conflicting Ownership Claims

When ownership is disputed or multiple individuals claim the same animal, the system should support administrative review.

The system must not automatically award ownership based on the earliest claim or an existing pet profile.

---

# 12. OPTIONAL PET REGISTRY

## 12.1 Registration Policy

Pet registration should be voluntary by default.

Mandatory registration should only be supported if an actual subdivision policy or applicable requirement establishes that obligation.

StraySafe should not independently create a new registration requirement.

## 12.2 Purpose

The Pet Registry should primarily provide:

- Convenient pet information storage.
- Faster lost-pet reporting.
- Supporting references for potential ownership identification.
- Information useful for relevant animal-related cases.

## 12.3 Suggested Pet Information

A pet profile may contain:

- Pet name.
- Species.
- Breed, if known.
- Color.
- Approximate size.
- Distinctive characteristics.
- Recent photographs.
- Owner-declared information.

Additional information should only be collected when justified by actual operational needs.

## 12.4 Registration Is Not Ownership Verification

Creating a pet profile establishes that a resident has declared an association with an animal.

It does not conclusively prove ownership.

A registered pet may resemble another animal.

Likewise, an unregistered animal may have a legitimate owner.

Therefore, pet registration must not be the sole basis for:

- Ownership confirmation.
- Violation attribution.
- Warning issuance.
- Penalty-related decisions.

## 12.5 Accessibility

Residents should remain able to report lost pets, submit animal incidents, and provide sightings without previously registering their animals.

If registration becomes mandatory under a confirmed policy, non-digital registration options should be considered for residents who cannot access the application.

# 13. ANIMAL IDENTIFICATION AND REPORT ASSOCIATION

## 13.1 Identification Challenges

Animals may have nearly identical physical characteristics.

For example, two brown dogs may have similar:

- Size.
- Coat color.
- Body structure.
- Breed.
- General appearance.

Consequently, the system cannot reliably establish that two sightings involve the same animal based solely on appearance.

## 13.2 Separate Incident Reports From Animal Identity

An incident report documents an observed concern.

An animal identity record represents a proposed or confirmed association with a specific animal.

These should not be treated as equivalent.

## 13.3 Possible Related Reports

The system may identify potentially related reports.

However, similar reports should not be automatically merged into one confirmed animal identity.

For example:

**Report A:** Brown dog observed on Street 2.

**Report B:** Brown dog observed on Street 4.

The reports may involve the same animal, different animals, or a broader recurring concern.

## 13.4 Recommended Association Approach

Preserve each original report.

Allow an administrator to associate reports when relevant.

Possible association classifications include:

- Possibly related.
- Confirmed related.
- Unrelated.

The system should also allow incorrect associations to be reversed.

## 13.5 Coordination Without Definitive Identification

Multiple reports may support one coordinated response without establishing that they involve the same animal.

For example, several reports of aggressive dogs within one area may justify coordinated safety assessment.

The subdivision should not need definitive animal identity before taking appropriate safety-related action.

## 13.6 Report Merging

Where consolidation is useful, the system should prefer logical grouping over destructive merging.

Original evidence, timestamps, reporters, and observations should remain intact.

This avoids losing information when an association is later found to be incorrect.

# 14. OWNERSHIP ASSESSMENT

## 14.1 Three Separate Questions

For animal-related nuisance cases, subdivision management should distinguish:

**Question 1:** Did the reported incident occur?

**Question 2:** Which animal was involved?

**Question 3:** Who, if anyone, is responsible for that animal?

Verification of one question does not automatically establish the others.

## 14.2 Potential Supporting Evidence

Ownership assessment may consider:

- Existing pet profiles.
- Previous photographs.
- Distinctive physical markings.
- Relevant CCTV footage.
- Firsthand resident observations.
- Previous documented interactions.
- Owner statements.
- Other appropriate supporting records.

No single type of evidence should automatically establish ownership in every situation.

## 14.3 Ownership Status

A simple proposed ownership classification is:

| Status       | Meaning                                                          |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unknown      | No identified potential owner                                    |
| Claimed      | A person has asserted ownership                                  |
| Under Review | Available ownership evidence is being assessed                   |
| Confirmed    | Ownership is sufficiently established for the relevant procedure |
| Disputed     | Ownership is contested                                           |
| Unconfirmed  | Available evidence is insufficient                               |

These classifications should not be confused with the overall incident case status.

## 14.4 Owner Denial

A suspected owner may deny ownership.

The system should record the statement and available supporting evidence.

Denial must not automatically close the case.

However, denial must not automatically be interpreted as dishonesty.

The subdivision should assess the available evidence according to its established procedures.

## 14.5 No Reliable Ownership Evidence

If ownership cannot be established, the case should remain an unidentified-owner concern.

Owner-specific warning procedures should not be imposed on an unsupported individual.

Appropriate incident assessment and coordination may still continue.

# 15. OWNER VIOLATIONS AND THREE-DAY OBSERVATION

## 15.1 Existing Process Context

Preliminary stakeholder information indicates that the subdivision has a three-day warning or observation procedure involving owned animals and owner-related violations.

The precise rules, authority, notification requirements, and escalation sequence require confirmation.

This proposal does not assume that every animal-related incident automatically qualifies for the three-day process.

## 15.2 Applicability

The procedure should only be applied when:

1. An applicable subdivision rule exists.
2. The relevant violation has been sufficiently established.
3. The responsible owner or party has been appropriately identified.
4. The procedural requirements for issuing a warning have been satisfied.

If the existing rule provides a three-day period, the system may record:

- Observation deadline.
- Owner response.
- Supporting evidence.
- Inspection records.
- Compliance outcome.

The exact countdown trigger must follow the confirmed policy.

It should not be assumed that the countdown begins on the date of the first complaint.

## 15.5 Repeated Violations

Previous incidents may provide relevant context.

However, repeated reports should not automatically establish repeated violations without appropriate assessment.

The system should preserve evidence and relevant administrative findings.

## 15.6 Extensions and Additional Warnings

The current workflow reportedly includes possible observation extensions and subsequent warnings.

These should be retained only according to the subdivision's confirmed procedure.

The system should not invent extension limits or escalation rules.

## 15.7 Fines and Penalties

Based on preliminary stakeholder information, fines are handled by the barangay rather than subdivision management.

Therefore, StraySafe should not impose, calculate, or collect barangay fines.

The subdivision may document relevant referrals and their reported outcomes.

# 16. UNIDENTIFIED OR OWNERLESS ANIMALS

## 16.1 General Principle

An animal without an identified owner should not automatically be treated as permanently ownerless.

It may be:

- A missing pet.
- An unregistered owned animal.
- A community animal.
- An abandoned animal.
- An animal with genuinely unknown ownership.

The system should preserve this uncertainty.

## 16.2 Proposed Response

When an unidentified animal is reported:

1. Receive the report.
2. Assess the concern.
3. Review available evidence.
4. Determine whether immediate intervention is necessary.
5. Consider community-assisted identification where appropriate.
6. Coordinate further assistance if warranted.
7. Document actions and outcomes.

## 16.3 Community Participation

A found-animal notice may help identify possible owners.

Residents may:

- Provide additional sightings.
- Identify a possible owner.
- Submit an ownership claim.
- Provide relevant information.

Participation should remain voluntary.

Residents should not be required to approach or capture an animal.

## 16.4 No Owner Identified

If no owner is identified, the subdivision may continue appropriate assessment and coordination.

However, the three-day owner compliance procedure should not automatically apply.

The absence of an owner does not justify inventing an owner-specific violation.

## 16.5 Physical Custody

Subdivision management should not be assumed to have responsibility for capturing, feeding, housing, or medically treating animals.

If physical intervention is necessary, coordination should occur with appropriately authorized and capable personnel.

The exact external referral procedure requires stakeholder confirmation.

---

# 17. DANGEROUS ANIMALS AND URGENT INCIDENTS

## 17.1 Urgent Concern Examples

Potential urgent incidents include:

- Aggressive animal behavior.
- Suspected rabies-related concerns.
- Animal attacks.
- Dangerous wildlife encounters.
- Situations involving immediate threats to residents.

## 17.2 Priority Handling

Urgent incidents should not wait for:

- Routine ownership identification.
- Pet registry matching.
- Community claims.
- Three-day observation.
- Ordinary administrative processing.

Appropriate safety coordination should begin promptly.

## 17.3 Resident Safety

The reporting interface should clearly discourage residents from approaching, capturing, provoking, or handling potentially dangerous animals.

Photographs should remain optional.

If a bite or possible rabies exposure occurs, residents should be directed to seek prompt medical assessment through appropriate healthcare services.

## 17.4 External Assistance

Subdivision management may coordinate with the barangay, trained animal handlers, veterinary personnel, or relevant wildlife responders.

The appropriate organization depends on the situation and applicable local arrangements.

## 17.5 Safety Communication

Where necessary, subdivision management may issue relevant community safety advisories.

Such advisories should communicate observed concerns and reasonable precautions without presenting unverified claims as confirmed diagnoses.

# 18. EXTERNAL AUTHORITY COORDINATION

## 18.1 General Arrangement

The subdivision head remains responsible for managing the subdivision's incident case.

External authorities may provide specialized assistance or intervention.

The subdivision should not assume authority over the external organization's internal operations.

## 18.2 Proposed Referral Information

A referral record may include:

- Associated incident.
- External organization contacted.
- Referral date and time.
- Reason for referral.
- Relevant evidence.
- Communication summary.
- Current coordination status.
- Agreed next action.
- Follow-up date, when applicable.
- Reported outcome.

## 18.3 Joint Consultation

The subdivision head and external authority may discuss appropriate intervention based on their respective responsibilities.

However, consultation does not automatically establish equal legal authority over animal custody or enforcement decisions.

The authorized party must remain responsible for actions within its legal mandate.

## 18.4 Subdivision Case Responsibility

External referral should not automatically close the subdivision case.

The subdivision head should continue monitoring the concern until an appropriate disposition is reached.

The system should record both independently.

# 19. SPECIAL SCENARIO: OWNERSHIP CLAIM DURING INTERVENTION

## 19.1 Scenario Description

A resident repeatedly reports an animal nuisance.

The subdivision documents the incidents but cannot reliably identify the owner.

After assessment, the subdivision coordinates with an external authority for an appropriate intervention.

When authorized personnel arrive, a person suddenly claims ownership of the animal.

## 19.2 Key Considerations

The late ownership claim may occur because:

- The person was unaware of previous complaints.
- The person only learned about the situation during the intervention.
- The person previously avoided acknowledging ownership.
- Other circumstances prevented earlier identification.

The system cannot reliably determine the claimant's motives.

A late claim alone should not establish dishonesty or liability.

## 19.3 Proposed Handling

**Step 1 — Record the claim.**

The ownership status changes to a pending or disputed state, as appropriate.

**Step 2 — Notify the relevant personnel.**

The subdivision head and external authority are informed of the new information.

**Step 3 — Reassess the planned intervention.**

The responsible parties consider the claim, available evidence, previous incidents, safety concerns, and applicable procedures.

**Step 4 — Determine the next appropriate action.**

Possible outcomes include:

- Allowing an established owner to secure the animal, where appropriate.
- Deferring intervention for further assessment.
- Continuing an authorized intervention when justified.
- Requesting additional assistance or review.

**Step 5 — Record the decision and reason.**

The subdivision administrator documents the coordination outcome.

## 19.4 Previous Reports Remain Valid Records

A new ownership claim must not erase previous incident reports.

However, previous reports should retain their actual evidentiary status.

They must not automatically become confirmed owner violations merely because ownership was later established.

## 19.5 Transition to Owner Compliance

If ownership and a relevant violation are sufficiently established, the case may transition to the applicable warning and compliance process.

The three-day period should follow the actual policy trigger.

It should not automatically be backdated to the first complaint.

## 19.6 Continued Case Management

Even when intervention plans change, the subdivision head remains responsible for documenting and monitoring the subdivision case.

The external referral history should remain preserved.

# 20. REPEATED ANIMAL NUISANCE REPORTS

## 20.1 Example Scenario

A resident repeatedly reports that a dog defecates near their gate.

The animal's owner is unknown.

Several reports contain similar descriptions and locations.

## 20.2 Proposed Handling

The system should preserve the individual observations while allowing them to be associated with an ongoing concern.

Repeated submissions should not automatically create separate enforcement actions.

## 20.3 Evidence Assessment

The administrator may review:

- Incident dates.
- Photographs or videos.
- CCTV footage, when legitimately available.
- Animal descriptions.
- Observation locations.
- Previous inspection findings.
- Potential ownership information.

## 20.4 Ownership Unknown

If the owner remains unidentified, the subdivision may pursue appropriate coordination without issuing unsupported owner-specific warnings.

## 20.5 Ownership Later Established

If ownership is later established, the administrator may review whether the documented conduct qualifies for the applicable owner compliance procedure.

The system must not automatically conclude that every previous observation involved the same animal.

# 21. CASE LIFECYCLE AND STATUS MANAGEMENT

A simple global case lifecycle is recommended.

## 21.1 Main Case Statuses

| Status       | Description                                             |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Reported     | The case has been submitted                             |
| Under Review | The case is being assessed                              |
| In Progress  | Relevant action, coordination, or monitoring is ongoing |
| Closed       | The case has reached a documented disposition           |

These statuses should remain distinct from specific operational activities.

## 21.2 Supporting Case Information

A case may separately record:

- Incident category.
- Urgency.
- Ownership status.
- Verification findings.
- Current action.
- Warning status.
- Observation deadline.
- Referral status.
- Follow-up requirements.
- Final disposition.

### Example A

**Case Status:** In Progress
**Incident Type:** Animal Nuisance
**Ownership:** Confirmed
**Current Activity:** Three-Day Observation
**Compliance:** Pending

### Example B

**Case Status:** In Progress
**Incident Type:** Unidentified Animal
**Ownership:** Unknown
**Current Activity:** External Coordination
**Referral:** Awaiting Response

This approach avoids creating an excessive number of global statuses.

## 21.3 Case Closure

Case closure should require an appropriate documented disposition.

Possible dispositions include:

- Concern resolved through intervention.
- Owner compliance verified.
- Missing pet recovered.
- Case formally handed off with no remaining subdivision action, where permitted.
- Report determined invalid.
- Report identified as duplicate.
- Case closed without confirmed resolution, with documented justification.

**Closed must not automatically mean rescued, removed, or successfully resolved.**

## 21.4 Cases Without Further Information

An animal may disappear from the reported location.

No further sightings may be received.

The subdivision may eventually determine that continued monitoring is no longer appropriate.

A case may then be closed according to a reasonable, stakeholder-approved procedure.

The system should preserve the reason for closure.

# 31. FINAL PROPOSED SYSTEM POSITIONING

StraySafe should be positioned as:

### An Animal Incident Reporting, Community Coordination, and Case Management System for Residential Subdivisions

Its primary purpose is to provide a structured mechanism through which animal-related concerns can be reported, assessed, coordinated, monitored, and documented.

The system combines two complementary operational functions:

**Community-Assisted Animal Identification and Recovery**

Supports lost-pet announcements, unidentified-animal notices, sightings, and relevant resident participation.

**Subdivision-Coordinated Incident Management**

Supports official incident assessment, applicable owner compliance procedures, external coordination, and case follow-through.

These functions should share relevant information without forcing all activities through the same administrative approval process.

### Core Operational Model

**REPORT → ASSESS → COORDINATE → MONITOR → DOCUMENT OUTCOME → CLOSE**

This model should remain flexible enough to accommodate:

- Unknown animal identities.
- Unconfirmed ownership.
- Moving animals.
- Incomplete evidence.
- Repeated incidents.
- Community-provided information.
- Ownership disputes.
- Changing intervention decisions.
- External assistance.
- Different animal species.
- Cases that cannot be fully resolved.

# 32. CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATION

The proposed direction for StraySafe is to prioritize reliable animal incident management and meaningful community coordination rather than attempt to automate every aspect of animal identification, enforcement, or rescue.

The system should recognize that real-world animal incidents frequently involve incomplete information, uncertain ownership, changing locations, and responsibilities shared across different organizations.

For this reason, StraySafe should preserve original observations, support evidence-based assessment, and allow appropriate actions to change as new information becomes available.

Subdivision management should remain responsible for managing subdivision-level cases, while specialized interventions remain with appropriately authorized external personnel.

Pet registration, artificial intelligence, and animal matching should function as supporting capabilities rather than mandatory foundations of the reporting process.

The three-day owner compliance procedure should be supported only according to verified subdivision policies and should not be applied indiscriminately to unidentified animals.

Community lost-and-found activities should remain accessible and convenient, allowing residents to share information without unnecessary administrative delays.

Most importantly, the system should distinguish between receiving a report, publishing community information, performing official intervention, and reaching a documented case outcome.

### Final Recommendation

**Adopt an incident-centric, community-supported, and subdivision-managed system architecture, subject to confirmation of existing policies, stakeholder responsibilities, and operational capabilities.**

The development team should review this proposal with the subdivision stakeholders, identify which procedures already exist, validate the proposed improvements, and approve the final business processes before undertaking major workflow refactoring.

**The objective is not to make StraySafe responsible for every animal-related problem. The objective is to ensure that the concerns falling within its approved scope can be handled through a clear, realistic, accountable, and maintainable process.**
